HealthFirst has completed its review of the first-level internal appeal regarding the prior authorization request for a prophylactic Anterior Cervical Discectomy and Fusion (ACDF) at the C5-C6 level (CPT 22551). After a comprehensive review of the clinical documentation, plan coverage criteria, California regulatory statutes, and generally accepted clinical guidelines, the decision to deny prior authorization has been UPHELD. 

Below is a detailed explanation of the clinical and legal basis for this determination.

I. CLINICAL SUMMARY
You are a 54-year-old male employed as a construction worker and heavy equipment operator. You present with a history of axial neck pain, rated at 5/10. A cervical spine MRI performed on September 15, 2024, demonstrated severe central canal stenosis at the C5-C6 level with a canal diameter of 6mm (normal is greater than 13mm). The MRI noted spinal cord contact without active compression, no T2 signal changes, and no evidence of myelomalacia. 

A comprehensive neurological examination performed on September 20, 2024, was completely normal. There were no signs of cervical radiculopathy (no dermatomal pain, numbness, or weakness) and no signs of cervical myelopathy (normal gait, no bilateral upper extremity weakness, normal reflexes, and negative Hoffman sign). 

Your conservative treatment history includes 8 weeks of supervised physical therapy (24 sessions) completed for axial neck pain, which resulted in modest pain improvement (from 7/10 to 5/10). You also completed a 3-month trial of Naproxen 500mg BID, a 6-week trial of Cyclobenzaprine, and received a C5-C6 epidural steroid injection on October 10, 2024, which provided 60% pain relief lasting 6 weeks. Your treating surgeon, Dr. Karen Davis, MD, requested prophylactic decompression, noting that your occupation as a heavy equipment operator increases your risk of cervical trauma, which could cause an acute spinal cord injury given the severely reduced canal diameter.

II. PLAN CRITERIA ANALYSIS
Under the HealthFirst Clinical Coverage Criteria for Spinal Surgery — Cervical Procedures (Document: CC-SPINE-2024), Section 2.1 outlines the diagnostic requirements for authorizing an ACDF (CPT 22551). The member must have a confirmed diagnosis of at least one of the following:
  (a) Cervical radiculopathy (nerve root compression with radicular symptoms);
  (b) Cervical myelopathy (spinal cord compression with myelopathic symptoms); or
  (c) Cervical disc herniation with objective neurological deficit.

Furthermore, Section 3.1 explicitly excludes coverage, stating: "Authorization will be DENIED when... Surgery is requested solely for axial neck pain without documented radiculopathy or myelopathy."

Because your clinical record and neurological examination on September 20, 2024, confirm that you have axial neck pain only, with no objective neurological deficits, radiculopathy, or myelopathy, the requested procedure does not satisfy the diagnostic requirements of Section 2.1 and is subject to the exclusion under Section 3.1 of CC-SPINE-2024.

III. EVALUATION OF THE PROPHYLACTIC ARGUMENT UNDER CALIFORNIA LAW
Your appeal emphasizes that prophylactic surgery is medically necessary to prevent a catastrophic, irreversible spinal cord injury (myelopathy) from potential minor trauma, particularly given your high-risk occupation as a heavy equipment operator.

We have evaluated this argument directly against California Health & Safety Code (CHSC) § 1374.32, which governs Independent Medical Review (IMR) standards in California. Under CHSC § 1374.32(a), "medically necessary" is defined to include health care services provided for the purpose of "preventing, evaluating, diagnosing, or treating an illness, injury, disease, or its symptoms."

However, CHSC § 1374.32(a)(1) strictly conditions this definition by requiring that any such preventive or therapeutic service must be "in accordance with generally accepted standards of medical practice." 

To determine whether prophylactic decompression for asymptomatic or axial-pain-only cervical stenosis is a generally accepted standard of medical practice, we refer to the clinical evidence base, specifically the AANS/CNS Joint Section on Disorders of the Spine Guidelines for the Management of Degenerative Cervical Myelopathy (2023 Update). Under Section 3.1 of these guidelines, surgical indications are classified into Tiers:
  - Tier 1 (Strong Indication): Moderate to severe myelopathy with objective cord compression, progressive neurological deficits, or myelomalacia (T2 cord signal change) on MRI.
  - Tier 3 (Surgery NOT Indicated): Axial neck pain without radiculopathy or myelopathy, or imaging findings without correlating clinical symptoms.

Because you have a completely normal neurological exam, no clinical symptoms of myelopathy, and no T2 signal changes or myelomalacia on your MRI, your clinical presentation falls under Tier 3 ("Surgery NOT Indicated"). Generally accepted standards of medical practice do not support performing an invasive, major spinal reconstruction (ACDF) solely for prophylactic risk reduction in an asymptomatic or axial-pain-only patient, even in the presence of a critically narrow 6mm canal. Consequently, the requested prophylactic surgery does not meet the statutory definition of medical necessity under CHSC § 1374.32(a).

IV. CLINICAL CATEGORY CLARIFICATION & RISK-BENEFIT ANALYSIS
We explicitly clarify and acknowledge a key clinical distinction regarding your conservative treatment:
  1. Conservative treatment (physical therapy and epidural injections) has successfully managed your axial neck pain. However, we recognize that conservative treatment is a pain-management modality; it cannot physically expand a 6mm bony canal, nor can it protect the spinal cord from traumatic impact. 
  2. The requested surgery is indeed intended for prophylactic risk reduction (preventing traumatic myelopathy) rather than axial pain management. 

Nonetheless, from a clinical risk-benefit perspective, surgical intervention (ACDF) carries significant, irreversible risks, including adjacent segment disease, pseudarthrosis, hardware failure, nerve injury, and esophageal injury. In the absence of active, objective neurological compromise (such as radiculopathy or myelopathy), the clinical standard of care to manage your structural risk is not prophylactic surgery. Instead, the generally accepted standard of care consists of strict activity modification (avoiding high-impact activities or modifying occupational duties to minimize trauma risk) and close clinical monitoring (regular neurological examinations and serial imaging to detect early signs of cord compromise).

Because there is no clinical evidence of active neurological deficit, and because prophylactic decompression for this presentation is not supported by national clinical guidelines, the prior authorization denial must be upheld.
